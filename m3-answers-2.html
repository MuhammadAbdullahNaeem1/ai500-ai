#Descriptive Statistics and Distributional Summaries

Q37. Define the arithmetic mean, weighted mean, and median. Under what distributional characteristics might each provide a useful summary?
Answer
  • Mean xˉ=1n∑xi\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_ixˉ=n1​∑xi​ — uses every value; efficient for roughly symmetric data without strong outliers; pulled by extremes.
  • Weighted mean xˉw=∑wixi/∑wi\bar x_w=\sum w_ix_i/\sum w_ixˉw​=∑wi​xi​/∑wi​ — when observations count unequally: survey weights, class weights, exposure durations, combining groups of different sizes. Its meaning depends on what the weights represent, so document them.
  • Median — middle ordered value; resistant to extremes; best for skewed data or data with influential outliers (income, waiting times, costs).

Neither is universally better; reporting mean and median shows skew through their gap.

Q38. Consider the observations {2, 3, 3, 4, 100}. Calculate the mean and median. Explain why the two statistics provide substantially different descriptions of the same dataset.
Answer

Mean =112/5==112/5==112/5= 22.4; median === 3. The mean incorporates the single extreme value 100, which pulls it far above the four values between 2 and 4 — so 22.4 describes none of the observations. The median depends only on the middle position and is resistant to the extreme value. The large gap signals right skew or an extreme observation (possibly an error) that should be investigated; here the median better represents the typical value.

Q40. Distinguish among variance, standard deviation, interquartile range, and median absolute deviation. Which measures are more resistant to extreme observations?
Answer
  • Variance: average squared deviation from the mean (squared units).
  • SD: square root of the variance (original units).
  • IQR =Q0.75−Q0.25=Q_{0.75}-Q_{0.25}=Q0.75​−Q0.25​: spread of the middle 50%.
  • MAD =median⁡∣xi−median⁡(x)∣=\operatorname{median}|x_i-\operatorname{median}(x)|=median∣xi​−median(x)∣.

Variance and SD square deviations, so one extreme value can dominate them. IQR and MAD are robust — they ignore how far the extremes are — and are preferred for skewed or heavy-tailed data (e.g. 10 waiting times: SD 23.9 with one 95-minute value vs 7.4 without; MAD 6 either way).

Q41. Explain the relationship among quantiles, quartiles, and percentiles. What information do the 25th, 50th, 90th, and 95th percentiles provide about a distribution?
Answer

A quantile QqQ_qQq​ has about a fraction qqq of values below it. A percentile is a quantile on a 0–100 scale (90th percentile =Q0.90=Q_{0.90}=Q0.90​). Quartiles are the three quantiles splitting data into four parts (Q0.25,Q0.5,Q0.75Q_{0.25},Q_{0.5},Q_{0.75}Q0.25​,Q0.5​,Q0.75​) — all quartiles are quantiles, not vice versa.

  • 25th and 75th: the middle 50% (their difference is the IQR).
  • 50th: the median — typical value.
  • 90th and 95th: the upper tail — the worst 10%/5% (service levels, waiting-time targets, screening extremes).

They need no distributional assumption, which suits skewed data.

Q43. Define skewness mathematically and explain the interpretation of positive, negative, and approximately zero skewness.
Answer

γ1=E[(X−μ)3]σ3\gamma_1=\dfrac{\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^3]}{\sigma^3}γ1​=σ3E[(X−μ)3]​ — the standardised third moment.

  • γ1>0\gamma_1>0γ1​>0: longer/heavier right tail (income, waiting times, healthcare costs); typically mean > median.
  • γ1<0\gamma_1<0γ1​<0: longer/heavier left tail.

Because deviations are cubed, the statistic is itself sensitive to extreme observations — read it with a histogram or boxplot.

Q44. Why can logarithmic transformations be useful for strongly right-skewed variables? Discuss their effects on distributional shape, interpretation, and subsequent statistical modeling.
Answer
  • Shape: log⁡(1+x)\log(1+x)log(1+x) compresses large values (each tenfold increase adds ≈2.3), so a long right tail becomes more symmetric and extremes lose dominance; +1+1+1 handles zeros.
  • Interpretation: the variable is no longer in original units; effects become relative/percentage changes — results must be explained or back-transformed.
  • Modelling: can stabilise variance, make relationships closer to linear (log–log visits vs sales), and reduce the influence of extremes — helpful for models assuming constant variance or linearity.

Use it only when the transformed scale is meaningful for the task, not merely to make a histogram look nicer; and apply the same transform at deployment (train–serving consistency).

Q45. Define covariance and explain how its sign and magnitude should be interpreted. Why are covariance values not directly comparable across variables measured in different units?
Answer

sXY=1n−1∑(xi−xˉ)(yi−yˉ)s_{XY}=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)sXY​=n−11​∑(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​). Sign: positive — large XXX tends to come with large YYY; negative — large XXX with small YYY; near zero — little linear co-movement. Magnitude: measured in the product of the units (kg·cm), so it depends on scale — converting cm to m divides it by 100 although the association is unchanged. Covariance magnitudes cannot be compared across pairs; use correlation for strength. (Diagonal of a covariance matrix = variances.)

#Relationships, Correlation, and Causal Interpretation

Q47. Distinguish among positive linear, negative linear, nonlinear, monotonic, threshold-based, interaction-driven, and outlier-driven relationships.
Answer
  • Positive / negative linear: YYY rises / falls at a roughly constant rate with XXX.
  • Non-linear: changes at a varying rate or direction (U-shape: age vs BP).
  • Monotonic: always increasing (or decreasing), though not at a constant rate (doubling curves) — captured by Spearman.
  • Threshold-based: little effect until XXX passes a cut-off, then a jump (AQI above a level).
  • Interaction-driven: the XXX–YYY relationship depends on a third variable (medication effect depends on age).
  • Outlier-driven: an apparent relationship created by one or a few extreme points.

Telling them apart needs a scatterplot, overall and within subgroups (Figure 32).

Q48. Why can Pearson correlation be close to zero even when two variables are strongly related? Illustrate using Y=X2Y=X^2Y=X2 for a distribution of XXX symmetric around zero.
Answer

Pearson measures only linear association. With X=−2,−1,0,1,2X=-2,-1,0,1,2X=−2,−1,0,1,2 and Y=X2=4,1,0,1,4Y=X^2=4,1,0,1,4Y=X2=4,1,0,1,4: the left half slopes down, the right half up, and the products (xi−xˉ)(yi−yˉ)(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​) cancel exactly, so r=0r=0r=0 — yet YYY is perfectly determined by XXX. A zero correlation means no linear association, not independence. Always plot first; use non-linear terms or other measures for such shapes.

Q50. Why might calculating Pearson correlation for ordinal categories encoded as integers produce a misleading interpretation?
Answer

Coding education as 1 = high school, 2 = bachelor's, 3 = master's, 4 = doctorate assumes equal distances between levels — that going from 1 to 2 is the same "amount" as from 3 to 4 — which the categories do not justify. Pearson then treats these arbitrary spacings as real numbers, so the coefficient's value depends on the coding. Use Spearman (only order matters) or polychoric correlation for ordinal variables; match the method to the measurement scale.

Q51. How can the correlation between two time-dependent variables be influenced by shared trends, seasonality, or temporal dependence?
Answer

If both series grow over time, or both peak in the same season, they correlate strongly even with no direct relationship — daily ad spend and sales both rise in holiday periods; ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer. Consecutive observations are also not independent, so the effective sample size is smaller than the row count and the correlation looks more certain than it is. Fix: detrend (correlate changes ΔXt=Xt−Xt−1\Delta X_t=X_t-X_{t-1}ΔXt​=Xt​−Xt−1​, or remove fitted trends/seasonality) and use time-series methods such as cross-correlation.

Q52. Explain why an aggregate association may become weaker, stronger, or reversed after stratifying the data into relevant subgroups.
Answer

The pooled association mixes two things: the relationship within each group and the differences between groups. If groups differ in both variables, the between-group difference can create, inflate, hide or reverse the within-group pattern (confounding, aggregation effects, Simpson's paradox). Example 68: exercise vs blood pressure is strongly negative overall, but weak in young adults, moderate in middle age and strong in older adults — partly because older adults both exercise less and have higher BP. An aggregate relationship need not describe any subgroup, so examine relationships overall and within meaningful groups.

Q53. Distinguish among confounding, reverse causation, selection effects, and chance as possible explanations for an observed association.
Answer

For an association between exercise (XXX) and blood pressure (YYY):

  • Confounding: a third variable drives both (diet, stress, healthcare access, age).
  • Reverse causation: YYY causes XXX (people with high BP exercise less because of their condition).
  • Selection effects: how units entered the data creates the association (people who choose to exercise differ systematically; only hospital patients studied).
  • Chance: random sampling variation, especially after examining many variables.

EDA can show the association but cannot tell which explanation is correct.

Q54. Why does correlation not establish causation? Explain how randomized interventions, causal models, and explicit causal assumptions can strengthen causal conclusions.
Answer

Correlation only shows that two variables move together; the same pattern is produced by causation, reverse causation, confounding, selection, measurement artefacts or chance (ice-cream sales and drownings both follow hot weather). Stronger evidence comes from:

  • Randomised interventions (RCTs, A/B tests): random assignment balances observed and unobserved factors, so outcome differences can be attributed to the intervention.
  • Causal models (DAGs): make assumed causal paths explicit, identifying confounders to adjust for and colliders not to.
  • Explicit assumptions (e.g. instrumental-variable conditions, no unmeasured confounding): state what must hold for an observational estimate to be causal, so it can be scrutinised.

#Feature Engineering, Scaling, Normalization, and Categorical Encoding

Q55. Define feature engineering and explain the meaning of ϕ(xraw)=xmodel\phi(x_{\text{raw}})=x_{\text{model}}ϕ(xraw​)=xmodel​. How can feature engineering improve the representation of information without necessarily introducing new information?
Answer

Feature engineering converts raw data into representations more useful for modelling. ϕ\phiϕ is the transformation (scaling, encoding, aggregation, mathematical transforms, new variables) mapping raw inputs xrawx_{\text{raw}}xraw​ to the model's inputs xmodelx_{\text{model}}xmodel​. It often re-expresses information already present in a form the model can use: a timestamp → hour of day or time since last event; date of birth → age at the observation; many transactions → recent transaction frequency. Nothing new is measured, but patterns (daily cycles, ageing, bursts of activity) become accessible — improving stability, encoding domain knowledge, expressing non-linearity and history.

Q56. Why must transformations whose parameters are learned from data be fitted using the training partition rather than the complete dataset?
Answer

If means, SDs, min/max, imputation values, category encodings, selected features or PCA directions are computed from the whole dataset, information from the test set influences the features used to build the model (leakage). The model is then evaluated on data that helped shape it, so performance is optimistically biased and will not hold on truly new data. Rule: ψ^=Fit(Xtrain)\hat\psi=\text{Fit}(X_{\text{train}})ψ^​=Fit(Xtrain​), then apply T(⋅;ψ^)T(\cdot;\hat\psi)T(⋅;ψ^​) unchanged to train, validation and test — ideally inside a modelling pipeline so each CV fold re-fits its own preprocessing.

Q59. Why are distance-based algorithms, regularized regression, and gradient-based optimization generally more sensitive to feature scaling than many tree-based models?
Answer
  • Distance-based (kNN, SVM, clustering): distances add squared differences, so a feature on a big scale (income up to 10710^7107) dominates one on [0,1][0,1][0,1] (debt ratio) purely because of its units.
  • Regularised regression: penalties act on coefficient sizes, and a coefficient's size depends on its feature's scale, so the same penalty affects features unequally.
  • Gradient-based optimisation: very different scales make the loss surface elongated, slowing or destabilising convergence.

Trees split on thresholds within one feature at a time ("is income > 50,000?"); rescaling a feature moves the threshold but not the split, so they are largely scale-invariant.

Q60. Distinguish among logarithmic transformations, square-root transformations, and nonlinear feature construction. How can each influence model behavior and interpretability?
Answer
  • Log log⁡(1+x)\log(1+x)log(1+x): strong compression of large values; reduces heavy right skew; coefficients become relative (percentage) effects.
  • Square root x\sqrt xx​: milder compression; useful for moderately skewed counts; stabilises variance of count data; interpretation in "root units" is less intuitive.
  • Non-linear feature construction (age², splines, age bands, interactions): lets a model represent curvature or thresholds (mortality rising steeply in old age), improving fit but making individual coefficients harder to explain and increasing overfitting risk.

All must be applied identically in training and deployment; prefer transformations whose scale stays meaningful.

Q61. Define binning and compare equal-width, equal-frequency, domain-defined, and supervised binning.
Answer

Binning maps a continuous variable to intervals using thresholds c1<⋯<cK−1c_1<\dots<c_{K-1}c1​<⋯<cK−1​.

  • Equal-width: intervals of the same width — simple, but skewed data leave most points in one bin and some bins empty.
  • Equal-frequency (quantile): each bin holds about the same number of points — balanced, but boundaries have no meaning and can be very uneven in width.
  • Domain-defined: thresholds with real meaning (AQI bands, clinical BP categories, policy limits) — preferred when such thresholds exist.
  • Supervised: cut-points chosen using the outcome — can capture thresholds well but risks target leakage; must be fitted on training data only.
Q62. What information can be lost when a continuous variable is converted into categories? Explain how artificial discontinuities arise near bin boundaries.
Answer

Binning throws away within-bin variation: systolic BP of 121 and 139 become the same category although clinically different, and fine gradients of risk disappear. It also creates artificial discontinuities: values just either side of a cut-point (139 vs 140) fall into different categories, so the model's prediction jumps at the boundary even though the underlying quantity barely changed — and small measurement noise near the boundary can flip the category (unstable predictions). Use bins only when thresholds are meaningful, and keep the continuous variable otherwise.

Q64. Why is target encoding especially vulnerable to target leakage? Explain how cross-fitting, out-of-fold estimates, smoothing, and explicit handling of unseen categories can reduce this risk.
Answer

Each row's encoded value is computed from outcomes that include its own, so the feature carries the label. For a category with one row, the encoding equals that row's outcome (lone Engineer who defaulted → 1.00). Training performance looks excellent and collapses on new data.

  • Cross-fitting / out-of-fold: split training data into folds; encode each fold using only the other folds' outcomes, so no row sees its own target.
  • Smoothing: shrink small categories towards the global mean, ncyˉc+λyˉnc+λ\frac{n_c\bar y_c+\lambda\bar y}{n_c+\lambda}nc​+λnc​yˉ​c​+λyˉ​​ (lone Engineer, λ=10\lambda=10λ=10: 6/11≈0.5456/11\approx0.5456/11≈0.545) — reduces noise but does not fully remove the row's influence, so combine with out-of-fold.
  • Unseen categories: a predefined fallback (global training mean or "unknown") for categories absent from training.

Q65. Define an interaction feature and explain the interpretation of the interaction coefficient in y^=β0+β1x1+β2x2+β3x1x2\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\beta_3x_1x_2y^​=β0​+β1​x1​+β2​x2​+β3​x1​x2​.
Answer

An interaction exists when the effect of one variable depends on the value of another. The feature x1x2x_1x_2x1​x2​ captures it. Differentiating, ∂y^/∂x1=β1+β3x2\partial\hat y/\partial x_1=\beta_1+\beta_3x_2∂y^​/∂x1​=β1​+β3​x2​: β1\beta_1β1​ is the effect of x1x_1x1​ when x2=0x_2=0x2​=0, and β3\beta_3β3​ is how much that effect changes per unit of x2x_2x2​. Justify and validate interactions; many of them add complexity and overfitting.

Q66. What are polynomial features, and how can they allow a model that is linear in its parameters to represent nonlinear relationships?
Answer

Polynomial features add powers (and products) of inputs: y^=β0+β1x+β2x2+⋯+βdxd\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2+\dots+\beta_dx^dy^​=β0​+β1​x+β2​x2+⋯+βd​xd; for two inputs at degree 2: x1,x2,x12,x22,x1x2x_1,x_2,x_1^2,x_2^2,x_1x_2x1​,x2​,x12​,x22​,x1​x2​. The model is still linear in the coefficients β\betaβ, so ordinary least squares and regularisation work unchanged, but the fitted function of xxx is a curve — capturing diminishing returns (spending vs income) or curvature. Low degrees remain interpretable.

Q67. Why can increasing the degree of polynomial features create problems involving multicollinearity, excessive dimensionality, unstable extrapolation, and overfitting?
Answer
  • Multicollinearity: xxx, x2x^2x2, x3x^3x3 are highly correlated, making coefficients unstable.
  • Excessive dimensionality: terms grow rapidly with variables and degree (degree 2 on 10 variables already gives 65 features).
  • Unstable extrapolation: high-degree polynomials swing wildly outside the training range.
  • Overfitting: a flexible curve fits noise; validation error rises.
  • Scale sensitivity: x5x^5x5 of large values explodes numerically.

Remedies: scale inputs, keep degrees low, regularise, and choose the degree by (cross-)validation.

Q69. A fraud detection system generates a prediction at time ttt. Explain why a rolling average constructed using observations from (t+1)(t+1)(t+1) or later would introduce temporal leakage.
Answer

At time ttt the system can only know transactions up to ttt. A rolling average that includes t+1t+1t+1 or later uses information that will not exist when the prediction is made — e.g. the burst of fraudulent transactions after the card was compromised. The model learns from the future, so development performance is artificially high, but the feature cannot be computed in deployment, so real performance collapses. Use backward-looking windows ending at ttt (or t−1t-1t−1), chronological splits, and documented feature timestamps; centred windows are only for retrospective description.

Q70. Why should cyclical variables such as hour of day, day of week, and month of year not always be represented using ordinary integer encoding?
Answer

Integer encoding puts the ends of the cycle far apart: hour 23 and hour 0 differ by 23, Sunday (7) and Monday (1) by 6, December (12) and January (1) by 11 — although they are adjacent in time. Distance-based and linear models then treat late night and early morning as opposites and cannot represent smooth patterns that wrap around midnight or the new year. A cyclical encoding preserves this circular proximity.

Q72. Identify and explain eight major feature-engineering risks: target leakage, temporal leakage, identity leakage, proxy discrimination, unstable high-cardinality features, train-serving inconsistency, excessive dimensionality, and spurious interactions.
Answer
  • Target leakage: a feature contains the outcome (target encoding on full data; "total defaults in the next 12 months").
  • Temporal leakage: a feature uses information from after the prediction time (centred rolling windows).
  • Identity leakage: rows of the same person/device in train and test, so the model recognises entities rather than learning patterns.
  • Proxy discrimination: a feature stands in for a sensitive attribute (ZIP code for race or income in lending).
  • Unstable high-cardinality features: IDs or rare categories memorised (customer ID) — no use for new entities.
  • Train–serving inconsistency: features computed differently in production (log income in training, raw income served).
  • Excessive dimensionality: too many engineered features (one-hot, polynomials) → overfitting and cost.
  • Spurious interactions: interactions that fit noise in training and do not replicate.

Document each feature's definition, unit, sources, window, availability time, missing-value rule, version and justification.
