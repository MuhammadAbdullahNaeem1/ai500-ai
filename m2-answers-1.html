#Data as a Constructed Record of Reality

Q1. Why is the phrase raw data considered misleading in data science and AI?
Answer

Raw data (notes §2.1): “The phrase raw data is a dangerous oxymoron.”

Data are produced, not found (notes §2.1.1): “Every dataset is actively produced through a long chain of subjective, technical, and systemic choices.”

Because data are never raw — they are always "cooked". Before any row is recorded, people have already decided what phenomenon matters and how to define it (conceptualization), what unit, population, instrument and frequency to use (observation), and what to record, how to encode missing values and how to store/link/transform it (engineering). "Raw" suggests an objective, pre-existing truth waiting to be harvested; in reality the dataset is an engineered artifact shaped by human assumptions, technical constraints and institutional priorities. Treating it as raw hides those choices and leads to models that inherit them unnoticed.

Q2. Explain why a dataset should be understood as an engineered artifact rather than a passive reflection of reality.
Answer

Data are produced, not found (notes §2.1.1): “Every dataset is actively produced through a long chain of subjective, technical, and systemic choices.”

Data as a map (notes §2.1.2): “Data is not a flawless mirror reflecting reality; it is a constructed map.”

A dataset is produced by a chain of choices: Dataset = Phenomenon + Measurement process + Sampling process + Institutional context + Data representation (Figure 14). Each component filters reality — the definition chooses what counts, the instrument decides what is observable, sampling decides who is seen, institutions decide the rules behind labels, and representation decides encoding and format. The values may look precise, but the concepts they stand for remain ambiguous. Example 38: a "dropout" dataset reflects university policy (one semester vs two years), scanner use, and main-campus-only sampling — not just student behaviour. So we must interrogate how the data were made before trusting what a model learns from them.

Q3. How do conceptualization, observation, sampling, institutional context and data representation jointly produce a dataset?
Answer
  • Conceptualization defines the phenomenon and the label (what "dropout" means).
  • Observation/measurement chooses unit of analysis, instruments and frequency (scanners vs manual registers).
  • Sampling decides who/what enters (main campus only; online, exchange, graduate and satellite students excluded).
  • Institutional context supplies the rules and incentives that generate records (dropout after one semester vs two years; billing rules in hospitals).
  • Data representation sets encoding and storage (NA vs 0, formats, linkage).
    Each step removes or reshapes part of reality, and the final spreadsheet is their combined product — it records the institution's procedures and systems as much as the people in it.
Q4. Why are labels such as disease, fraud, dropout, risk and success not self-defining?
Answer

Their meaning is fixed by operational rules, human judgement and institutional practice, not by the word. "Dropout" could be permanent exit, failure to enrol next semester, transfer, approved leave or late graduation — each gives different students different labels (Example 38). "Disease" may follow a billing code rather than clinical severity; "fraud" depends on what investigators confirm; "risk" on the chosen horizon and threshold. Hence the target is constructed, not discovered, and must be explicitly defined and documented.

Q5. How can two institutions use the same variable name while assigning fundamentally different meanings to it?
Answer

Each institution generates the field under its own rules. In Example 38, University A labels a student dropout after one missed semester, University B only after two years. Both columns are called dropout, but they encode different policies. Similarly Hospital X codes every complication for reimbursement while Hospital Y codes only the primary diagnosis. Merging such data without understanding their origins produces misleading training data — the model learns institutional differences as if they were differences in people. Fix: data dictionaries with precise definitions, provenance, and harmonization before pooling.

Q6. Explain how operational definitions influence the target labels used in supervised learning.
Answer

A supervised model learns P(Y∣X)P(Y\mid X)P(Y∣X) for the YYY that was operationalized. Change the definition and you change which cases are positive: defining dropout as "fails to register next semester" also labels transfers, approved leave, temporary financial problems and administrative delays as dropouts (Scenario S1). The model then learns to predict that rule — including its errors and institutional artefacts — and its accuracy is measured against it. So the operational definition determines what the model actually predicts, its class balance, its fairness, and whether it supports the intended decision.

Q7. What does the formulation Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C) communicate about dataset quality?
Answer

Fitness for purpose (notes §2.1.2): “There is no such thing as a universally good or high-quality dataset in a vacuum. A dataset is only good for a specific purpose under specific conditions.”

Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C) = the fitness of dataset DDD for task TTT under context CCC. It says quality is not an intrinsic, universal property of a dataset but a relation between the data, the purpose and the setting — aligned with NIST, which judges quality and representativeness relative to intended use (accuracy, completeness, consistency, relevance, timeliness). The same student dataset scores high for annual reporting but low for a 6-month early-warning system, a national model, or a causal question.

Q8. Why can a dataset be suitable for descriptive reporting but unsuitable for causal inference?
Answer

Descriptive reporting only needs approximate counts/associations of what happened ("what % of first-years left?"). Causal inference needs to separate the effect of an intervention from confounders. Observational data show that library users drop out less, but library users may also be more motivated, better prepared, wealthier, better supported (Example 38, Task 4). Without measuring/controlling these, the data reveal association, not causation. The data are adequate for T1T_1T1​ and inadequate for T4T_4T4​ — exactly the Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C)Q(D,T,C) point.

Q9. How can an exclusion decision during sampling affect the population to which a model can validly be applied?
Answer

A model is only validated for the population its data represent. Collecting only main-campus undergraduates (excluding online, exchange, graduate, satellite-campus students) means the model is representative of one population; applying it to the excluded groups — or nationwide — gives unreliable predictions, because Pdata(X,Y)≠Ptarget(X,Y)P_{\text{data}}(X,Y)\ne P_{\text{target}}(X,Y)Pdata​(X,Y)=Ptarget​(X,Y) (covariate/label/concept shift). Exclusions must be documented and the model's intended use restricted to the covered population, or new data collected.

Q10. Explain why the correct question is not "Is this data good?" but "Is this data fit for the intended task and context?"
Answer

Fitness for purpose (notes §2.1.2): “There is no such thing as a universally good or high-quality dataset in a vacuum. A dataset is only good for a specific purpose under specific conditions.”

Data as a map (notes §2.1.2): “Data is not a flawless mirror reflecting reality; it is a constructed map.”

Because no dataset is good in a vacuum — quality is contextual. A dataset may be fine for descriptive statistics but fatally flawed for causal inference; useful retrospectively but too late for real-time prediction; representative of an urban hospital but useless for a rural clinic; valid for adults but not children; good for classification but poorly calibrated for absolute risk. Data are a constructed map: we must know how it was drawn and whether it is scaled for our journey. Asking "fit for TTT in CCC?" forces us to check timeliness, representativeness, definitions and governance for the actual decision.

#Data Sources and Their Inherent Realities

Q11. What are the principal advantages of surveys as a source of data for AI systems?
Answer

Surveys (notes §2.2.1): “Surveys collect information by prompting respondents with structured or semi-structured questions …”

Surveys can capture latent constructs that rarely appear in routine databases: beliefs, preferences, intentions, self-reported behaviours, social circumstances and perceived quality of life. They are designed by the researcher — target population, questions, wording and sampling are controlled — and can be weighted to match a population (e.g. national age/sex distributions). They are often the only defensible route to measure opinion (e.g. a nationally representative survey vs social media in S2).

Q12. Distinguish among coverage error, sampling error, nonresponse bias, recall bias, social-desirability bias and instrument effects.
Answer

Coverage error (notes §2.2.1): “The sampling frame misses parts of the target population.”

Sampling error (notes §2.2.1): “The selected sample differs from the population purely by chance.”

Nonresponse bias (notes §2.2.1): “Respondents differ systematically from those who refuse to participate.”

Recall bias (notes §2.2.1): “Inaccurate memory of past events.”

Social-desirability bias (notes §2.2.1): “Respondents provide answers they believe are socially acceptable rather than truthful.”

Instrument effects (notes §2.2.1): “Responses shift based on question wording, sequencing, or cultural/linguistic translation.”

Q13. How can a model trained on survey data learn reporting behaviour rather than the intended real-world construct?
Answer

Survey AI implication (notes §2.2.1): “A model trained on survey data may learn response behavior rather than the intended construct …”

The label is self-reported, so it equals actual behaviour × willingness/ability to report it. Systematic differences in reporting become signal: if more-educated respondents report accurately while others overstate, the model may associate education with activity purely through reporting accuracy (Example 39). It learns how people answer questions. When applied to objective data (wearable trackers measuring movement), performance degrades because the target has changed from reported to actual behaviour.

Q14. Explain the roles of systematic bias, random noise, environmental conditions and device characteristics in sensor measurements.
Answer

Sensors (notes §2.2.2): “Sensors convert physical phenomena into recorded digital signals …”

Sensor takeaway (notes §2.2.2): “Sensor data may appear highly objective, but the physical instrument and the acquisition process fundamentally shape what is observed.”

Basic model: Xobs=Xtrue+b+ϵX_{\text{obs}}=X_{\text{true}}+b+\epsilonXobs​=Xtrue​+b+ϵ — bbb systematic bias (persistent offset, e.g. a miscalibrated monitor reading +20 mmHg) and ϵ\epsilonϵ random noise (zero-mean fluctuation). Realistic model: Xobs=g(Xtrue,Z,θdevice)+ϵX_{\text{obs}}=g(X_{\text{true}},Z,\theta_{\text{device}})+\epsilonXobs​=g(Xtrue​,Z,θdevice​)+ϵ, where ZZZ = environmental conditions (temperature, lighting, interference) and θdevice\theta_{\text{device}}θdevice​ = device-specific behaviour (model, firmware, calibration). So the reading depends on the instrument and setting, not only the true quantity — "objective" sensor data are shaped by acquisition.

Q15. What data-quality problems can result from calibration drift, clock misalignment, packet loss, firmware changes and signal saturation?
Answer
  • Calibration drift → growing systematic error (accuracy), readings not comparable over time.
  • Clock misalignment (device vs server time) → wrong event order, temporal leakage and wrong feature windows (timeliness/consistency).
  • Packet loss → missing values/intervals, often not at random (completeness).
  • Firmware changes → sudden distribution shift halfway through collection (consistency; R3's heart-rate example).
  • Saturation/clipping → true extremes recorded as the maximum, hiding the tails that matter most (accuracy).
    Plus: does zero mean zero or missing? Were sites comparable?
Q16. Why should a social media platform be treated as part of the measurement instrument when web data are collected?
Answer

Platform as measurement (notes §2.2.3): “Observed web behavior is not a direct measure of stable human preference. It is a reaction within an engineered environment, defined by a combination of user, interface, ranking algorithm, moderation, and platform incentives.”

Because the observed behaviour is produced inside an engineered environment: what users see depends on the recommendation algorithm, what remains depends on moderation, what they post depends on trending topics, interface design and social incentives (likes, shares). A post is a response to the platform, not a direct expression of stable belief. Unlike a survey, where researchers design the questionnaire, here the platform is the questionnaire. Example 40: a change to favour short video cut text posts — a platform change, not an opinion change.

Q17. How can recommendation algorithms, moderation policies, bots and duplicate posts distort conclusions drawn from web data?
Answer
  • Recommendation algorithms amplify emotionally charged, polarized content → moderate views under-collected.
  • Moderation removes some posts → what's left is filtered.
  • Bots/coordinated campaigns post identical messages thousands of times → automation mistaken for public sentiment.
  • Duplicates/reposts: one post shared 100,000 times counts one person's opinion 100,001 times → frequencies inflated.
    Net effect: estimated support reflects amplification and automation, not the number of unique people who hold a view; models learn platform-specific patterns.
Q18. Distinguish among event time, recording time, arrival time and processing time in system logs.
Answer

Event time (notes §2.2.4): “When the real-world action actually occurred.”

Recording time (notes §2.2.4): “When the local system logged it.”

Arrival time (notes §2.2.4): “When it reached the central data pipeline.”

Processing time (notes §2.2.4): “When the pipeline actually handled it.”

Event time — when the real-world action occurred. Recording time — when the local system logged it. Arrival time — when it reached the central data pipeline. Processing time — when the pipeline handled it. They can differ by seconds to weeks (retries, offline devices, batch jobs). Building features or splits on the wrong one is a primary cause of temporal leakage and incorrect feature windows: for prediction, a feature counts only if it was available by the prediction time.

Q19. Why do administrative records often represent institutional behaviour and access patterns rather than objective ground truth?
Answer

Administrative records (notes §2.2.5): “Administrative records are generated to support organizational operations, not scientific research …”

Administrative data (notes §2.2.5): “Administrative data encodes institutional behavior not objective reality.”

Administrative formulation (notes §2.2.5): “Observed outcome is a function of Underlying Condition, Access, Policy, and Institutional Practice.”

They are created to run organizations (billing, reimbursement, enforcement), not to measure reality: Observed outcome = f(Underlying condition, Access, Policy, Institutional practice). A diagnosis code may reflect billing optimization; an arrest reflects policing patterns; spending reflects access to care; absences may reflect transport/caregiving; unemployment records omit informal workers. Example 41: a remote patient who never returns has no readmission record — not because they recovered; Hospital A's overnight-observation policy creates admissions Hospital B never records. Records capture utilization, not burden of disease.

Public datasets (notes §2.2.7): “Public datasets are released for research, education, benchmarking, or policy use …”

#Public and Synthetic Data

Q21. Why does public availability not guarantee that a dataset is accurate, representative, ethically usable or free of legal restrictions?
Answer

Public datasets (notes §2.2.7): “Public datasets are released for research, education, benchmarking, or policy use …”

"Public" describes access, not quality or permission. A public dataset may be outdated, have unclear provenance, hidden preprocessing, undocumented exclusions and label errors (Northcutt et al. found ≥3.3% average test-label error); it may not represent a real deployment population; it may carry privacy risks; its licence may forbid commercial use or redistribution; and the original consent may not cover new uses (Example 57). So every public dataset still needs provenance, quality, licence and consent checks.

Q22. What is benchmark saturation, and how can repeated use of a public test set weaken the credibility of model comparisons?
Answer

Benchmark saturation: models become overfitted to a public test set after years of reuse, so scores stop reflecting real generalization. Even without training on test labels, researchers inspect errors and redesign models: test results → research decisions → new models → same test set. The test set becomes part of development (a form of leakage), so small improvements may be noise or label-error artefacts. Remedies: hidden or rotating test sets, fresh data, external validation, limited submissions, post-deployment evaluation.

Q23. How can label errors in a benchmark test set alter the ranking of competing ML models?
Answer

Reported accuracy is computed against observed labels Y~\tilde YY~, not true labels YYY: when Y~i≠Yi\tilde Y_i\ne Y_iY~i​=Yi​, a correct prediction is scored wrong and a wrong one may be scored right. When top models differ by less than the number of mislabeled test cases, rankings are determined by noise. High-capacity models that learn fine distinctions may disagree with wrong labels and look worse, while simpler models that reproduce annotation patterns look better. Northcutt et al. showed rankings changed after correction — agreement with benchmark labels ≠ agreement with reality.

Q24. Why should public datasets undergo continuous auditing, correction, versioning and maintenance?
Answer

Because a dataset is never finished: label errors, ambiguity, outdated categories and new uses surface over time. Auditing (e.g. Confident Learning + human review) finds errors; correction restores trustworthy evaluation; versioning (D(1.0),D(1.1),D(2.0)D(1.0),D(1.1),D(2.0)D(1.0),D(1.1),D(2.0), reported in every paper) keeps old and new results comparable and reproducible; maintenance provides an error-reporting pathway, owners and an update schedule. Treating benchmarks as immutable truth let errors persist (case 2.11.3) — "the inevitability of maintenance".

Q25. What are the main methods used to generate synthetic data?
Answer

Synthetic data (notes §2.2.8): “Synthetic data consists of artificially generated observations designed to mimic the statistical properties of real data.”

Rule-based simulators (traffic simulator with fixed rules), mechanistic models (climate physics equations), agent-based models (epidemic with interacting agents), GANs (generator vs discriminator; synthetic faces), VAEs (decode from a latent space; MNIST-like digits), diffusion models (learn to reverse noising; text-to-image), LLMs (synthetic conversations/text/code), and statistical sampling from fitted distributions (fit a normal to blood pressure and sample).

Q26. Identify six useful applications of synthetic data in ML and data engineering.
Answer
  1. Simulating rare events (rare complications, failures). 2. Privacy-preserving data access (develop models without identifiable records). 3. Augmenting under-represented classes / balancing datasets. 4. Testing data pipelines and software before deployment. 5. Training in unsafe or expensive environments (robots, vehicles). 6. Counterfactual scenarios ("what if average stay were two days shorter?"). (Example 43 uses privacy, balancing, testing and counterfactuals.)
Q27. Why does synthetic data not automatically guarantee privacy or anonymity?
Answer

Synthetic data (notes §2.2.8): “Synthetic data consists of artificially generated observations designed to mimic the statistical properties of real data.”

The generator is trained on real records and can memorize them: some synthetic records may be near-copies of real people, enabling membership inference (was this person in the training data?) and reconstruction of sensitive attributes. Rare combinations are especially identifying. Generated health data often retains residual disclosure risk. Hence privacy must be tested (membership-inference attacks, record-similarity/nearest-neighbour distance checks) — synthetic does not mean anonymous or risk-free.

Q28. Define fidelity, utility, privacy, fairness, coverage and realism as validation dimensions for synthetic data.
Answer

Fidelity (notes §2.2.8): “Does it preserve relevant distributions and relationships?”

Utility (notes §2.2.8): “Does it actually support the intended analysis or model training?”

Privacy (synthetic) (notes §2.2.8): “Can original individuals be inferred or reconstructed?”

Fairness (synthetic) (notes §2.2.8): “Are subgroup properties preserved appropriately without amplifying bias?”

Coverage (notes §2.2.8): “Are rare but critical edge cases represented?”

Realism (notes §2.2.8): “Do the generated combinations obey real-world domain constraints?”

  • Fidelity — preserves relevant distributions and relationships (age distribution, disease frequencies, correlations).
  • Utility — supports the intended analysis (model trained on synthetic performs similarly on real data).
  • Privacy — original individuals cannot be inferred or reconstructed.
  • Fairness — subgroup properties preserved without amplifying bias.
  • Coverage — rare but critical edge cases represented (severe sepsis, uncommon drug reactions).
  • Realism — combinations obey domain constraints (no male patient marked pregnant).
Q29. How can synthetic data reproduce or amplify biases contained in the original source data?
Answer

The generator learns the source distribution — including its gaps and historical patterns. If young chronically ill patients were under-represented, it produces too few of them (Example 43), so a downstream model works for the elderly and poorly for the young. Generators tend to reproduce the dense, common regions and smooth away rare subgroups and tails, and they faithfully copy biased labels (e.g. discriminatory historical loan approvals, R4). Matching overall distributions can therefore hide subgroup erasure and amplify disparity.

Q30. What evidence would be required before a model trained on synthetic clinical data could be trusted on real patients?
Answer
  • Validation on real, representative, held-out patient data (not a synthetic test set) — discrimination and calibration.
  • Subgroup and intersectional performance (age, sex, ethnicity, sites), including rare conditions (coverage).
  • Synthetic-data fidelity checks (distributions, correlations, tails) and realism constraints.
  • Privacy audit of the generator (membership inference, memorization).
  • External validation at other hospitals and, ideally, prospective/silent deployment with monitoring.
  • Documentation (datasheet for the synthetic data) and governance approval.

Q33. Why can a dataset with 99 percent populated cells still be incomplete for a particular application?
Answer

Completeness (notes §2.3.1): “Completeness measures whether all required data is actually present.”

Cell counts measure missing cells, not missing records, time intervals, subgroups, modalities, labels, metadata or provenance. A dataset can be 99% populated while entirely excluding an important or vulnerable population (e.g. rural or online students), lacking a key modality (mental-health indicators), or missing the timestamps needed for prediction. For a task that needs those, it is incomplete. Completeness is judged by: does the missing information prevent reliable use for the intended purpose?

Q35. Why does agreement among values within a database not establish that the values are correct?
Answer

Accuracy caution (notes §2.3.2): “Agreement within a database does not, by itself, establish correctness.”

Internal agreement only shows consistency. Values can agree and all be wrong: a miscalibrated monitor reads every patient +20 mmHg (consistent, inaccurate); copied or duplicated errors repeat; a wrong record linkage propagates; annotators trained with the same flawed guideline agree with each other. Accuracy requires comparison with an independent, trustworthy reference outside the database.

Q36. Distinguish among formatting, logical, structural and cross-system consistency.
Answer

Consistency (notes §2.3.3): “Consistency asks whether data values agree logically across formats, records, systems, and established rules.”

  • Formatting: same meaning, different encodings — "Male/M/1/man"; DD/MM vs MM/DD dates; "Yes/Y/1/True/Diabetic".
  • Logical: violates real-world rules — discharge (Jan 10) before admission (Jan 15); death before a later visit; child older than parent; p∉[0,1]p\notin[0,1]p∈/[0,1].
  • Structural: mixed units in one column (70 kg vs 155 lb), duplicate IDs for different people, conflicting labels for one entity across files.
  • Cross-system: the same entity/value recorded differently in different systems (e.g. two hospitals' EHRs) — needs reconciliation.
Q37. Explain the differences among freshness, latency, update frequency and validity period.
Answer

Freshness (notes §2.3.4): “How old the underlying information actually is.”

Latency (notes §2.3.4): “The delay between the real-world event occurring and the data becoming available in the system.”

Update frequency (notes §2.3.4): “How often the values are refreshed.”

Validity period (notes §2.3.4): “How long a specific data point remains operationally relevant.”

Freshness — how old the information is. Latency — delay between the event and its availability (diabetes in January, recorded in April → 3 months). Update frequency — how often values are refreshed (finances once per semester). Validity period — how long a value stays operationally relevant (a cholesterol value valid ~6 months). A fraud system needs millisecond latency; annual planning tolerates months.

Q38. What is feature staleness, and how can it reduce the reliability of real-time predictions?
Answer

Timeliness (notes §2.3.4): “Timeliness concerns whether data is sufficiently current and available exactly when it is needed for a decision.”

Staleness: at prediction time ttt, feature age Δt=t−tlast update\Delta t=t-t_{\text{last update}}Δt=t−tlast update​; if Δt\Delta tΔt exceeds a task-specific threshold, the feature is stale. (Cholesterol from January used in October: Δt=9\Delta t=9Δt=9 months > 6-month guideline.) Stale features describe a past state — the model predicts on outdated information (e.g. assumes a patient is non-diabetic), reducing accuracy and calibration, and creating a train–serving mismatch if training used fresher values.

Q39. Explain the difference between covariate shift, label distribution shift and concept shift.
Answer

Representativeness (notes §2.3.5): “Representativeness asks whether the data adequately reflects the intended target population and the actual operating conditions of deployment.”

  • Covariate shift: P(X)P(X)P(X) changes, P(Y∣X)P(Y\mid X)P(Y∣X) stable — model moved to a hospital with different demographics or practices.
  • Label distribution shift: P(Y)P(Y)P(Y) (class proportions) changes — fraud 1% in training, 10% at deployment → poorly calibrated.
  • Concept shift: P(Y∣X)P(Y\mid X)P(Y∣X) — the mapping itself — changes — purchasing behaviour after a recession.
Q40. Why does a very large sample size not compensate for systematic underrepresentation of important populations?
Answer

Representativeness (notes §2.3.5): “Representativeness asks whether the data adequately reflects the intended target population and the actual operating conditions of deployment.”

More data from an already well-represented group shrinks variance for that group but adds no information about the missing ones. Table 15: 80% of a million patients are 65+ while the population is 25% 65+; adding another million elderly does nothing for the 18–40 group. Systematic gaps are bias, not noise — size cannot average them away. Intersectional gaps (500 young women, 500 elderly men) also persist. #Measurement Error and Construct Validity

Q41. Distinguish between random and systematic measurement error.
Answer

Measurement error (notes §2.3.6): “Measurement error is the fundamental difference between an observed value and the true underlying construct or quantity it is intended to measure.”

Random: X∗=X+ϵX^*=X+\epsilonX∗=X+ϵ, E[ϵ]=0\mathbb E[\epsilon]=0E[ϵ]=0 — scattered around the truth (140 recorded as 138, 141, 142, 139); increases variance, weakens associations, no bias in the centre. Systematic: X∗=X+b+ϵX^*=X+b+\epsilonX∗=X+b+ϵ, b≠0b\ne0b=0 — persistent directional shift (140,130,120 recorded as 150,140,130); produces biased estimates and flawed conclusions.

Q42. Why can averaging reduce the effect of random error but fail to remove systematic error?
Answer

Averaging nnn readings gives Xˉ∗=Xˉ+b+ϵˉ\bar X^*=\bar X+b+\bar\epsilonXˉ∗=Xˉ+b+ϵˉ. Because E[ϵ]=0\mathbb E[\epsilon]=0E[ϵ]=0, ϵˉ→0\bar\epsilon\to0ϵˉ→0 as nnn grows (errors −2,+1,+2,−1 cancel), so random error shrinks. But bbb is the same in every reading, so it survives averaging unchanged: an uncalibrated monitor is still +10 mmHg on average. Only calibration against a reference removes bbb.

Q43. What is differential measurement error, and why is it important for fairness analysis?
Answer

Error rates that differ across subgroups: P(X∗≠X∣A=a)P(X^*\neq X\mid A=a)P(X∗=X∣A=a) varies with a sensitive attribute AAA. If one group is measured much less accurately, the model's inputs (or labels) are noisier or biased for that group → lower and unequal performance, which the model cannot mathematically correct from the data alone. It produces measurement bias and must be checked by subgroup when auditing fairness.

Q44. How can a medical sensor that performs differently across skin tones create unequal model performance?
Answer

A wearable heart-rate sensor with ±2 bpm error on light skin and ±10 bpm on dark skin (notes' example) gives the model much noisier inputs for darker-skinned patients. Thresholds and patterns learned mostly from accurate readings misfire on noisy ones → more false alarms/missed deteriorations for that group. If such readings also define labels, errors enter the target. Result: differential measurement error → measurement bias → unequal accuracy, invisible unless performance is reported by skin tone.

Q45. Define construct validity in the context of data collection and AI development.
Answer

Construct validity (notes §2.3.6): “The most dangerous measurement error occurs before the instrument is even used: the chosen variable simply does not measure the intended concept.”

Construct validity is the degree to which a measured variable or label faithfully represents the concept we actually care about. Its failure — "the ultimate error" — happens before any instrument is used: the chosen variable measures something else (cost for need, arrests for crime, clicks for satisfaction, exam scores for intelligence, absence for disengagement). Then the model becomes a highly optimized system for measuring the wrong thing.

Q46. Why can healthcare spending be a poor proxy for health need?
Answer

Spending =f(need,access,insurance,provider behaviour,historical inequality)=f(\text{need},\text{access},\text{insurance},\text{provider behaviour},\text{historical inequality})=f(need,access,insurance,provider behaviour,historical inequality). Two patients with identical need incur different costs when one faces access barriers (Table 17: both high need, $30,000 vs $4,000). A model trained on cost concludes the low-spending patient has lower need. Obermeyer et al.: at the same score, Black patients were sicker than White patients because less had historically been spent on them. Accurate cost prediction ≠ accurate need prediction.

Q47. Why can arrest records be a poor proxy for actual crime commission?
Answer

An arrest requires detection, investigation and enforcement — shaped by policing intensity, patrol allocation, surveillance, reporting behaviour and institutional practice. Two people with the same likelihood of offending differ in arrest probability if one lives in a heavily patrolled area (Example 64). Many offences are never detected; some arrests don't involve offending. Arrests measure enforcement, and feed feedback loops (more patrols → more arrests → more patrols).

Q48. Why can user clicks be a poor proxy for genuine user satisfaction?
Answer

Clicks reflect what the interface showed and how (ranking position, thumbnails, clickbait, autoplay), curiosity, accidental taps and engagement-maximizing design — not whether the user was satisfied afterwards. Platform algorithms amplify emotionally charged content that is clicked but disliked. Optimizing clicks can therefore reduce satisfaction while metrics rise — a construct-validity failure in which the platform is part of the measurement instrument.

Q49. Explain why model optimization cannot correct a target variable that measures the wrong construct.
Answer

Construct validity (notes §2.3.6): “The most dangerous measurement error occurs before the instrument is even used: the chosen variable simply does not measure the intended concept.”

Optimization minimizes error with respect to the given target YYY. If YYY is a biased proxy for the construct Y∗Y^*Y∗, a better optimizer just predicts the proxy more accurately — including its distortions (C^≈C\hat C\approx CC^≈C does not imply C^≈H\hat C\approx HC^≈H). "The algorithm works exactly as designed. It is simply optimizing the wrong target." The data contain no signal telling the model it is off-target; only redefining the target (or validating the proxy) fixes it.

Q50. How should domain experts evaluate whether a proposed proxy faithfully represents the concept of interest?
Answer
  • State the true construct and trace the chain construct → institutional process → recorded proxy → training target.
  • List what else drives the proxy (access, insurance, policy, policing, interface).
  • Validate against direct measures on a sample (chronic conditions, physiological indicators, clinician-reviewed need).
  • Check group invariance: compare E[H∣C=c,A=a]\mathbb E[H\mid C=c,A=a]E[H∣C=c,A=a] across groups; check subgroup calibration P(H≥h0∣S=s,A=a)P(H\ge h_0\mid S=s,A=a)P(H≥h0​∣S=s,A=a).
  • Involve clinicians, ethicists, affected communities in approving the target; document the proxy and its limitations; monitor outcomes after deployment.
